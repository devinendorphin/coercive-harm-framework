# Research: emotional and psychological abuse tactics in the clinical literature

*Compiled 2026-10-04 at the operator's direction, before the in-order SWW read continues
past Season 6. It is a "continuing education" pass. It covers which perpetrator tactics
the clinical and criminological literature treats as frequent; what it says about
separation; and three operator corrections to the close-read: what "low variance" means,
triangulation, and non-romantic love bombing. It complements
`taxonomy-external-grounding.md` (DARVO, Biderman, Stark) and does not repeat it.*

## 0. The correction this file exists to carry

**"Low variance" refers to the perpetrator's behaviours, not to the stories or the
outcomes.** Until now the close-read's case tables mixed three different things: the
perpetrator's conduct, the target's responses (leaving, fawning, documenting), and
outcomes (exposure, a death, a conviction, survival). Variance in the second and third is
**expected**, and it is not evidence against the claim. The most important reason is
this. The target's attempt to separate is the point at which the perpetrator's control is
threatened. The literature (§2) identifies separation as a period of sharply elevated
danger, so outcomes *at* that point will scatter widely: escape, retaliation, years of
litigation, homicide, suicide. Outcome variance is what a constant behaviour-set looks like
when it meets different resources, institutions and luck.

Consequence for the coding (applied in `sww-corpus/close-read/CODEBOOK.md`, amendment 1):
**C5 ("discard") was being scored ✗ whenever the target left first. That was wrong.** A
target's exit is a target response, not the absence of a perpetrator behaviour. What the
perpetrator does *in response to* that exit is coded instead.

## 1. What the instruments measure: the frequent tactics

These are the constructs with measurement behind them, as distinct from practitioner
models.

| Source | Construct / factors | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Follingstad et al. 1990** (*J. Family Violence* 5:107), 234 women in physically abusive relationships | Six types of emotional abuse: **threats of abuse, ridicule, jealousy, threats to change the marriage (leave or affair), restriction of freedom, damage to property**. 99% reported emotional abuse. **Ridicule** was most often named the worst | `[ESTABLISHED]` as a descriptive typology; sample: physically abused women |
| **Murphy & Hoover 1999**, Multidimensional Measure of Emotional Abuse (MMEA) | Four factors: **Restrictive Engulfment** (isolation, jealousy, monitoring), **Denigration** (humiliation, degradation), **Hostile Withdrawal** (silent treatment, withholding affection), **Dominance/Intimidation** (threats, property violence). The structure is confirmed by factor analysis and cross-validated in several languages | `[ESTABLISHED]` |
| **Tolman 1989/1999**, Psychological Maltreatment of Women Inventory | Two factors: **dominance–isolation** and **emotional–verbal** abuse | `[ESTABLISHED]` |
| **Stark 2007**, *Coercive Control* | **Isolation, intimidation, degradation, micro-regulation of everyday life** (clothing, food, movement, money, contact). Framed as a "liberty crime". The basis of the England & Wales s.76 offence | `[ESTABLISHED]` as a framework; operationalised in statute |
| **Dutton & Painter 1993** (*Violence & Victims*), 75 women who had recently left | **Traumatic bonding**: attachment to the abuser is predicted by **intermittent maltreatment and power imbalance**. Relationship variables explained 55% of the variance in attachment six months after leaving | `[SUPPORTED]`. Explains C1/C2 alternation and why leaving is hard |
| **Harsey & Freyd** 2017/2020/2023 | **DARVO**. In experiments, exposure makes observers rate the victim as less credible and more responsible | `[SUPPORTED]`. See `taxonomy-external-grounding.md` |
| **Sweet 2019** (*Am. Sociol. Rev.*), 43 women from DV support groups | **Gaslighting is sociological, not only psychological.** Perpetrators mobilise gender stereotypes and institutions, and **enlist family, mutual friends and institutions to corroborate a false account**, repeatedly over time | `[SUPPORTED]`. The key citation for C4 and triangulation (§3) |
| **Crick & Grotpeter 1995** (*Child Dev.*) | **Relational aggression**: harming a person through their relationships (rumour, exclusion, threats to withdraw friendship). Developed on children; the construct generalises | `[ESTABLISHED]` construct; adult and coercive application `[SUPPORTED]` |
| **Leymann 1990/1996**, mobbing (LIPT, 45 acts) | Workplace: attacks on communication, social relations, reputation, occupational situation, health. **Five phases**: critical incident → targeted abuse → **management intervenes, often siding with the perpetrators** → **target labelled "difficult" or "mentally ill"** → expulsion | `[SUPPORTED]` (Leymann's prevalence and suicide figures are contested). Maps onto C2→C4→C5 at work |
| **Strutzenberg et al. 2017**, 484 young adults | **Love bombing**: excessive early communication and attention to gain influence. Correlated with narcissistic traits and both anxious and avoidant attachment, and negatively with self-esteem. "First study to empirically examine" it | `[HYPOTHESIS → thin SUPPORTED]`. One student sample; romantic only |
| **Duluth Power & Control Wheel** (Pence & Paymar 1993) | Practitioner synthesis: intimidation, emotional abuse, isolation, minimising/denying/blaming, using children, economic abuse, male privilege, coercion and threats | Practitioner model, `[SUPPORTED]` as a descriptive map, not validated as an instrument |
| **Hassan, BITE model** | Behaviour, Information, Thought and Emotional control in high-control groups. Includes **information rationed by rank** and **distrust of critics and ex-members** | Practitioner model. Critics note the indicators are loose; used here only for the information-control items |

**The pattern across the instruments.** Isolation and monitoring, denigration, hostile
withdrawal, intimidation, and reality distortion turn up in every validated measure. That
convergence across independent instruments, samples and decades is the strongest external
evidence that the *perpetrator's* behaviour-set is narrow. `[SUPPORTED]`. The instruments
measure **frequency**. None of them tests the operator's specific claim that the
behaviours **co-occur as one ordered cycle** across settings. That remains
`[HYPOTHESIS]`, which is what the close-read is for.

## 2. Separation: why outcome variance is expected

- **Wilson & Daly 1993** (*Violence & Victims* 8:3). Using all police-known spousal
  homicides in Canada (1974–90), New South Wales (1968–86) and Chicago (1965–90), **wives'
  risk of being killed was substantially higher when separated than when co-residing**.
  The female bias in victimisation was greater after estrangement. Risk is highest
  immediately after estrangement but persists for months or years. `[ESTABLISHED]`
- **Campbell et al. 2003** (*AJPH* 93:1089), an 11-city case-control study of 220 intimate
  partner femicides against 343 abused controls. Independent risk factors include
  **estrangement, especially from a controlling partner**, a gun, prior threats with a
  weapon, and a stepchild in the home. The incident-level factor was the victim leaving
  for another partner. Stalking, forced sex and abuse in pregnancy were bivariate risks.
  `[ESTABLISHED]`. Note "especially from a controlling partner": control plus separation,
  not separation alone.
- **Femicide Census (UK).** Among women killed by an ex-partner, reported proportions
  killed **within the first month of separation range ~30–55% by year (38% pooled
  2009–18), and ~70–87% within the first year**. `[SUPPORTED]`. These are *conditional*
  shares (among those killed after separating), not the probability that a separating
  woman is killed. They vary by report year.
- **Monckton Smith 2020/2021**, 372 UK intimate-partner homicides. An eight-stage timeline:
  (1) a history of control or stalking → **(2) romance develops quickly** → (3) coercive
  control → **(4) a trigger that threatens control, such as separation** → (5) escalation
  → (6) a change in thinking → (7–8) planning and homicide. Secondary summaries differ on
  the labels of stages 6–8, and the primary page returned HTTP 403. Its finding that
  coercive control predicts homicide better than prior violence matches Campbell.
  `[SUPPORTED]`. Retrospective and constructed from homicides, so it shows the path *to*
  lethal outcomes, not how often that path is completed. **Stage 2 is the C1 love bomb, and
  stage 4 is the target's exit.**
- **Spearman, Hardesty & Campbell 2022** (*J. Adv. Nurs.*). Defines **post-separation
  abuse**: legal abuse, economic abuse, threats and endangerment to children, **isolation
  and discrediting**, harassment and stalking. Essential attributes include **"manipulation
  of systems"**. Consequences include lethality and "institutional violence and betrayal"
  (loss of custody, economic deprivation). `[SUPPORTED]`

**Disconfirming check on the operator's wording.** The claim "the act of trying to
separate… lots of times results in the death of the person trying to leave" needs one
qualification. The *relative* risk at separation is established. The *absolute* risk for
any one leaver is low: most separations from abusive partners do not end in homicide. The
accurate statement is that separation is the period of **highest** risk, **most** intimate
partner homicides of separated women cluster in it, and control plus separation is the
signature risk combination. The operator's structural point holds in full: outcome
variance at exit is expected, and exit is when the perpetrator's behaviours intensify.
**Post-separation abuse is the C6 / C4 phase seen from the clinical side.**

**In the corpus so far:** S1 recast Sarah's exit as "a huge mistake" and hoovered with
refunds and account logins. In S3, exposure and an ultimatum were followed within hours by
the co-perpetrator's suicide; lethality at the separation point, self-directed. S5b: car
keys held as a threat, the "good-faith" $5k demand, "I can't control what he will do to
you". S5c: after the children were removed, flying monkeys, workplace tracking, the parents
appearing at a bus stop, presents and letters. S6 J.E. has no intimate separation, but the
stalking escalated in a second wave after a lull.

## 3. Triangulation and information control (operator's specification)

The operator describes two forms. Both are **control of information between other
people**, which Sweet (2019) and relational-aggression research place at the centre of
abuse. Both go further than Bowen's family-systems "triangulation", which is a general,
often non-malicious way of offloading anxiety onto a third person. For this repo, the
coercive forms are coded separately:

**T1, the mediator-poisoner.** A offers to "talk to C for you" and support B in a
disagreement, then speaks negatively about B to C. A becomes the only channel between B
and C and corrupts it in both directions.
*Corpus:* S3 Patty, a ten-year back-channel to the daughter-in-law with fabricated relays
("Victoria said you were rude") and an "information-laundering chain" (Victoria → Patty →
Ted → aunt). S5b: Kelly's husband told Kelly her mother disliked her and told the mother
Kelly hated her. S5c: Rachel's mother offered to broker contact with Daniel, conditional on
the father being present, while telling Daniel "Rachel chose not to talk to you."

**T2, serial tailored smear plus anti-comparison conditioning.** Separate conversations
with each member of the target's support system. Each portrays the target badly in terms
**tailored to that listener's values**, after first assessing them. The listeners are
conditioned **not to compare notes**, because comparing would collapse the inconsistent
accounts.
*Corpus:* S2 T: "She kept everybody in a box so that none of us intersected… a whole
separate set of lies for each." S3: Ted told Victoria's brother she was "crazy", told Brad
she was a monster, and the daughter-in-law was told she lies. S5b: the in-laws were told
Kelly was the abuser; leadership said "we do not talk about Kelly and X's relationship," a
**rule against comparing notes issued at the institutional level**. S4: "you didn't dare
talk to your spouse"; enforced denunciation.
*Literature:* Sweet's "enlisting others to corroborate", relational aggression, Leymann's
attacks on reputation, the BITE information items. The **tailoring per listener** and the
**rule against comparing** are less directly measured. They are described in practitioner
literature on "abuse by proxy" (National DV Hotline and similar) but I found no validated
instrument item for them. `[HYPOTHESIS, corpus-supported]`. This is a gap the framework
could fill (§5).

**Why it matters for evidence.** T2 is designed to be invisible to any one witness. Each
listener holds a plausible, internally consistent story. **The proof is the comparison
across accounts**, which is exactly what the perpetrator conditioned people against. This
is the documentation burden again: the target must reconstruct a lie-structure spread
across people who were told not to talk to each other.

## 4. Love bombing is a function, not a romance

The only empirical study is romantic (Strutzenberg 2017). The operator's point is that C1
is defined by **function**: a binding investment that creates obligation, dependency or
access. Romance is only one of its forms. Forms in the literature and the corpus:

| Setting | Form | Grounding |
|---|---|---|
| Romantic | rapid escalation, "romance develops quickly" | Strutzenberg 2017; Monckton Smith stage 2 |
| Group / cult | the honeymoon of unconditional acceptance, then love conditional on performance | Hassan (read on air, S4); Singer; S4, S5a |
| Workplace | the favoured protégé, special access or mentorship, then a sudden fall | Leymann's critical incident follows a prior relationship; S2 (in reverse: the subordinate need-bombs the senior) |
| Friendship | ambient, years-long inclusion | S3 Patty |
| Need-bombing | the perpetrator's manufactured need (illness) binds the helper | S2 (fabricated cancer), S6a (fictional dead child, secondhand) |
| Offered provision | the perpetrator meets the target's need (housing, food) | S6 host ("eat dinner with us") |
| **Polycule / metamour** | a partner's other partner idealises or befriends, then triangulates within the network | **No research literature found.** `[HYPOTHESIS]`. Watch for it in later seasons |
| **Housing: the lodger who won't leave** | an informal arrangement, often out of need or kindness, becomes occupation. Removing the occupant carries a large cost | **"Cuckooing" / home takeover** (UK safeguarding and research: Greater London Authority 2023; Macdonald et al., Durham): "localised or opportunistic" and **"quasi-cuckooing", where the victim initially consents**, then is controlled through coercion, emotional abuse, financial exploitation and forced dependency. `[SUPPORTED]` for vulnerable adults |

**On the cost of extraction.** In some jurisdictions occupancy itself converts a guest into
a protected tenant. In California, about 30 days' residence can give month-to-month tenant
protections, so removal requires formal notice and an unlawful-detainer suit (practitioner
and legal-guide sources; `[SUPPORTED]`, verify per jurisdiction before relying on it). That
is the **law's default turned into leverage**, the same structure as S5b, where a withheld
marriage licence left Kelly with no marital property rights. The "huge cost" of removal is
partly built by the legal system, which the perpetrator can use and the target must pay
for.

## 5. What this changes in the framework

1. **Codebook (done; amendment 1):** low variance is scored on perpetrator behaviours
   only. Target responses and outcomes are recorded but are not tests. C5 is recoded so
   that a target's exit is never "✗". A new field, **R-SEP, records the perpetrator's
   response to separation or exposure**. T1/T2 and C1-form are coded explicitly.
2. **doc 02 (taxonomy):** candidate entries T1 and T2, conduct-based ("what would a
   witness see": the same person giving differently framed negative accounts of the target
   to different listeners, plus instructions not to discuss the target with others).
   Requires `/liberty-review`: "a smear campaign" is also what a falsely accused person will
   allege (DARVO in reverse). Corroboration across listeners is the safeguard.
3. **doc 04 (evidence):** T2 is proven by **cross-account comparison**. The evidentiary
   standard should treat assembled, mutually inconsistent accounts given to different
   listeners as pattern evidence. This links to `documentation-labor-burden.md`.
4. **Risk:** the separation-risk findings belong in `foreseeability-and-risk-assessment.md`
   (control plus separation; Monckton Smith stage 4).

## BOUNDARY

This note establishes which perpetrator behaviours recur across validated instruments and
independent samples, and that separation is a period of elevated risk. It does **not**
establish that the behaviours form a single ordered cycle. It does not establish the
absolute risk of homicide for any individual leaver. The love-bombing evidence is one
student study, and T2's "tailoring" and "no comparing notes" have no validated measure.
The non-romantic forms of C1 are grounded in corpus instances and adjacent literatures
(cults, mobbing, cuckooing), not in a love-bombing literature.
