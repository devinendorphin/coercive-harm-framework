## 1. Primary source (verbatim)

Murphy, Jane M. (1976). "Psychiatric Labeling in Cross-Cultural Perspective." *Science*
191(4231): 1019–1028. doi:10.1126/science.1251213.

The whole paper is an **argument against** (a) Scheff-style labeling theory — the claim
that "mental illness" is merely norm-deviation a society chooses to stigmatize — and
(b) strong cultural relativism. Murphy's use of kunlangeta is in service of that
argument: even an isolated non-Western society names a psychopath-like *conduct*
pattern AND holds it distinct from both ordinary bad acts and from treatable
mind-disorder. Reading the term outside that argumentative frame is the first error the
pop chain makes.

**Definition (verbatim, p. 1021):**
> "the Eskimos have a word, *kunlangeta*, which means 'his mind knows what to do but he
> does not do it.' This is an abstract term for the breaking of many rules when
> awareness of the rules is not in question. It might be applied to a man who, for
> example, repeatedly lies and cheats and steals things and does not go hunting and,
> when the other men are out of the village, takes sexual advantage of many women —
> someone who does not pay attention to reprimands and who is always being brought to
> the elders for punishment."

**Prevalence + the "ice" line (verbatim, p. 1021):**
> "One Eskimo among the 499 was called kunlangeta. When asked what would have happened
> to such a person traditionally, an Eskimo said that probably 'somebody would have
> pushed him off the ice when nobody else was looking.' This suggests that
> permissiveness has a limit even in a cultural group which in some respects ... is very
> lenient."

**Not curable / not illness (verbatim, p. 1021):**
> "It is of considerable interest that kunlangeta and arankan are not behaviors that the
> shamans and healers are believed to be able to cure or change. As a matter of fact,
> when I pressed this point with the Yoruba healers they specifically denied that these
> patterns are illness."

**The contrast category (this is the load-bearing find), *nuthkavihak* (pp. 1021–1022):**
The Eskimo word for what they translated as "being crazy" — "something inside the
person — the soul, the spirit, the mind — is out of order." Manifestations Murphy lists:
talking to oneself, screaming at someone who does not exist, believing a relative was
murdered by witchcraft when nobody else believes it, believing oneself to be an animal,
refusing to eat, becoming violent, killing dogs. **Crucially: "For both nuthkavihak and
were [the Yoruba parallel] indigenous healing practices are used."** So the culture ran
*two* categories with *opposite* treatment logics: nuthkavihak (mind-disorder → shamans
treat it) vs. kunlangeta (conduct with intact awareness → shamans are *not* believed
able to change it, and it is not classed as illness).

**Arankan (Yoruba parallel, verbatim):** "a person who always goes his own way regardless
of others, who is uncooperative, full of malice, and bullheaded." (Note spelling: Murphy
writes **arankan**. The "aranakan" variant in some secondary sources is not in Murphy
1976 — treat as unverified.)

## 2. Fieldwork basis / methods (the part the pop chain omits)

From Murphy's "Method of Study" (p. 1020, verbatim):
> "The data ... derive mainly from a year of field work, in 1954-55, in a village of
> Yupik-speaking Eskimos on an island in the Bering Sea ... Some of the Eskimo data came
> from a **key informant**, who systematically described the life experiences of the 499
> Eskimos who constituted a total village census over the 15 years previous to and
> including the year of investigation."

What this establishes, and what it does **not**:
- **Geography correction.** Murphy says "an island in the Bering Sea," and elsewhere
  references "a related Eskimo settlement in Siberia." These are **Siberian Yupik**
  (a.k.a. St. Lawrence Island Yupik), whose kin are in Chukotka — a *different language*
  from mainland Central Alaskan Yup'ik. The common gloss "northwest Alaska" (traceable to
  Martha Stout's popularization) is **imprecise-to-wrong**. St. Lawrence Island is the
  overwhelmingly likely specific site (only US Bering Sea island of Siberian Yupik with
  Siberian kin), but Murphy does not name it in this paper — tag the specific island
  [SUPPORTED, needs primary confirmation]; the "Siberian Yupik, not mainland NW Alaska"
  correction is firm from Murphy's own words.
- **Single-ethnographer, largely single-key-informant.** Much of the Eskimo conduct data
  runs through one key informant characterizing 499 people over a 15-year census window.
- **n = 1 applied case.** Exactly one person in the 499 was actually called kunlangeta.
  The definitional illustration ("always being brought to the elders") is Murphy's
  *general* gloss of the term, not necessarily a record of that one person's biography.
- **The "ice" line is a hypothetical.** It answers "what *would have* happened ...
  traditionally," and the informant hedges it ("*probably*"). It is a single informant's
  speculative reconstruction of a pre-contact response — **not** documentation that any
  kunlangeta was ever killed. The pop-psychology retellings (and, in softer form, the
  current docs/11 entry) present it as settled traditional practice; it is not.

## 3. Independent attestation — a real gap [RESEARCH NEEDED]

- **No independent lexical attestation located.** The word does not appear in the
  English Wiktionary; no St. Lawrence Island Yupik / Siberian Yupik dictionary entry was
  found in this pass. Central Alaskan Yup'ik lexicography (Jacobson) covers a *different*
  language and would not settle it. As far as this session can verify, "kunlangeta" lives
  in the record almost entirely through Murphy 1976 and the citation chain downstream of
  it. This does not mean the word is spurious — mid-century single-ethnographer terms
  often are genuine — but it means the term is **effectively single-sourced**, and the
  entry's confidence should reflect that.
- **The distortion chain to correct:** Murphy (1976) → Ned Kalin / academic citations →
  Martha Stout, *The Sociopath Next Door* (2005) → viral usage (incl. 2022 Boris Johnson
  memes). Each hop hardens "probably pushed off the ice" into "the Inuit executed
  psychopaths," which the primary text does not support.

## 4. Colonial / exoticization check

Recorded 1954–55 by an American psychiatric anthropologist, mid-century power relations,
leaning on one key informant; deployed then and now to argue a thesis in *Western*
psychiatry (the cross-cultural reality of "psychopathy" against labeling theory). No
Yupik-internal source — corroborating or contesting Murphy's gloss, the term's range, or
the "ice" claim — was located in this pass. The term's modern life is almost purely as
Western pop-psychology shorthand, detached from any living Yupik usage we can cite. Do
not present the term as a window into "how the Inuit think about evil"; present it as
*one ethnographer's 1950s finding*, valuable but thinly sourced.

## 6. Witch-trial audit (feeds docs/07 and docs/11 Open Questions)

The kunlangeta *category* and the kunlangeta *response* must be scored separately:
- The **category** (a named, conduct-based, awareness-intact pattern held distinct from
  illness) is the framework-friendly find and survives scrutiny as *one* data point of
  cross-cultural recognition.
- The **response** ("pushed off the ice ... when nobody else was looking") is the
  archetype of everything /liberty-review exists to block: covert, unadjudicated,
  irreversible, consensus-triggered, no evidentiary standard, no appeal, no safeguard
  against the accusation itself being the coercive act. It is a **negative anchor**, not
  a precedent. Murphy's own paper elsewhere treats witchcraft accusation as a
  transgression-labeling system — the same machinery that elsewhere persecutes innocents.

## 7. Modern implementation counterpoint

The strongest empirical rebuttal to the incorrigibility verdict baked into the "ice"
answer is the **Mendota decompression model** (docs/11 Part C): high-PCL:YV youth show
roughly halved violent recidivism under intensive, correctly-designed treatment. That
evidence is **youth-only**; adult evidence is far weaker (Salekin's "clinical lore"
critique cuts both ways). So: the traditional "not curable" judgment may reflect
*available technology + adult population + small-society cost structure* (a village of
≤499 genuinely could not absorb a persistent predator) rather than an ontological fact
about the pattern. **The category-recognition travels; the untreatability verdict does
not.**
