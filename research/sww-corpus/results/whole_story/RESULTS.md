# Whether institutions protected the people who came to them

*2026-11-11. Whole-story analysis of Something Was Wrong, seasons 1–26. This work remains
on the unmerged `analysis/whole-story-isolated` branch. [SUPPORTED] below describes the
coded podcast stories; it does not establish a population rate.*

**Few of the agreed stories ended in full institutional protection: 79 of 389, or 20.3%.**
That is below the pre-registered 40% line, including after the larger framing band is applied.
The two other pooled predictions are **not decided by this method**: the share encountering
disbelief, blame, dismissal or institutional self-protection lies too close to 30%, and the
share whose protection depended decisively on work outside the institution lies too close
to 50%. These are three different findings; a lack of full protection does not by itself
show disbelief, blame or institutional bad faith.

The weakest measurement condition is the broken blinding for every Claude coder. The harness
showed them the project’s instructions. Claude results are **unblinded**; Codex results are
**blinded**. The framing experiment measures an extra sentence on top of that exposure for
Claude, and only one season for Codex. Neither establishes that all measurement bias is small.

## What was tested, and how to read the numbers

The unit is a **person’s story about one harm, within one season**, including children,
co-victims, updates and listener stories. Separate harms by different perpetrators remain
separate. A second analysis retains each identifiable person–harm story at its earliest
appearance across seasons, before testing eligibility or agreement. It cannot identify every
repeat, and it is not an exact count of unique human beings.

Headline fractions include matched records where both coders qualify for that prediction
and agree on its indicator. The own-coder fractions include all that coder’s eligible
records, including unmatched ones. Left/right means A/B in Claude seasons, CA/CB in Codex
seasons. All seasons uses Claude’s original S17 pair once; the Codex S17 substitution is shown
separately. No coder output or identity match was changed to obtain a result.

[ARITHMETIC.md](ARITHMETIC.md) gives all season figures, field agreement, own-coder results,
Wilson intervals, central-survivor checks, full-protection-only checks, exclusions and
framing variants. [summary.json](summary.json) retains exact ratios and omitted denominators.
[QUOTATION-AUDIT.md](QUOTATION-AUDIT.md) records the contrary-case and quotation checks.

## Could full protection have been missed?

**Disconfirming test:** look for concrete institutional action that made someone safe,
including later updates; see whether own-coder figures, repeat removal, contradiction
exclusion, quotation exclusion or the other S17 pair overturn the result.

The contrary examples are real. In S5, both Claude **unblinded** coders record social services
obtaining custody for Daniel’s sister. In S9, both Codex **blinded** coders include Danielle’s
later custody outcome: “I have custody of my daughter. He gets zero visitation. He has no
rights.” This is stronger counter-evidence than an isolated scene where an official listened.
The words were checked against the update transcript, S9 update part 2, around 37:19.

At the same time, the S1 Sarah records show why useful action need not be full protection.
The bank completed refunds within 24 hours and counseling helped, while her family organized
her departure. Those helpful responses remain in the coding. In S7, the blinded Codex coders
split over Amy’s ten-year order and later statement that abuse continued: one calls it full
protection, the other partial. For Hadas, later prosecution cannot make a murdered person safe.

The fixed sample checks **12 coded records, six matched stories**, for this question.
Counting identifiable stories once yields 76/357 (21.3%) pooled; excluding quotation-flagged
records yields 75/378 (19.8%); combining both yields 72/346 (20.8%). Excluding contradictions
alone leaves the primary fractions unchanged. The deceased-with-protection exclusion gives
78/387 (20.2%). Every fixed sensitivity keeps the full-protection rate below 40% and the
prediction not refuted, in both families and in the S17 substitution. Full protection is
uncommon in this coded sample; that is stronger than a claim that no institution helped.

## Could the adverse-response result be an artifact of whose stories were counted?

**Disconfirming test:** distinguish inaction from disbelief or blame, inspect an agreed
negative case and a disputed case, and compare the separate families and central survivors.

Inaction alone does **not** enter this indicator. Sarah’s bank and counseling contacts show
help and omissions without a coded response of the four specified kinds. Kelly’s S5 account
provides the opposite case: both Claude **unblinded** coders record counseling that dismissed
her sexual-abuse disclosure, threats over a financial arrangement and church suppression of
her account. Her description of the counselor’s threat—“if you don’t do this, I can’t control
what he will do to you”—is present in the S5 E5 transcript around 30:40. No legal finding
about the counselor is inferred from the quotation.

The disputed examples show a measurement boundary. In S4, Claude’s **unblinded** coders
differ over whether authorities used against a Temple member count when his coercion was
not reported to them. In S10, Codex’s **blinded** coders both record religious harm to Mia,
but only one codes the church/college teachings as adverse institutional contacts. The
passage does describe those teachings; it does not establish a disclosure to those authorities.
A quotation can be accurate while the contact classification is debatable.

Central-survivor-only figures are 58/115 = 50.4% pooled, 27/53 = 50.9% for Claude
**unblinded**, and 32/63 = 50.8% for Codex **blinded**. This matters: the pooled 30.8% is not a
stable rate across story roles. These checks do not replace the all-person headline. The
fixed contrary-case sample is **12 coded records, six matched stories**.
Repeat removal gives 109/350 (31.1%) pooled. Quotation exclusion gives 109/370 (29.5%);
combining the two gives 100/337 (29.7%). Each remains **not decided** after its recalculated
framing band. Claude-only **unblinded** repeat removal changes its verdict from refuted to
**not decided** (40/147, 27.2%); quotation exclusion gives 35/146 (24.0%, refuted). Codex-only
**blinded** figures remain not refuted in the fixed sensitivities. The primary per-season
verdict stays on record, alongside this fragility.

## Did protection depend mainly on the person and their network?

**Disconfirming test:** look for protection driven by institutions themselves, and test
whether attributing credit is consistent when institutions and families both did essential work.

Jez’s S6 story is a contrary case: restaurant staff recognized a deceptive dating setup,
warned her and helped her leave safely. Both Claude **unblinded** coders credit the institution.
Kenji’s S9 employer investigated and cleared him; both Codex **blinded** coders credit the
institution for that partial protection. These are retained at equal weight with failures.

The paired examples also show genuine attribution difficulty. For J.E.’s S6 threats, one
Claude **unblinded** coder credits the FBI identifying the caller, the other the mother’s
recordings and escalation; both passages are present in the updates. For Jessa’s S11 story,
Codex’s **blinded** coders emphasize different protections: academic accommodation versus
containment of a fugitive threat. The latter decisive quotation also describes the pressure
of a relative about to reveal his whereabouts. It does not cleanly establish institutional
initiative. Natalia’s S13 account records substantial work gathering evidence, finding an
address and refusing an agreement without a court record, alongside active legal aid and
court protection. A report alone is not supposed to earn the reporter decisive credit.

The fixed sample is **12 coded records, six matched stories**. On full protection alone,
outside decisive work is 20/56 = 35.7% pooled, 9/24 = 37.5% for Claude **unblinded**, and
12/33 = 36.4% for Codex **blinded**. Central-survivor figures and own-coder figures are also
below 50%, but attribution uncertainty and the framing rule prevent a pooled verdict.
Repeat removal gives 48/112 (42.9%) pooled; quotation exclusion gives 49/110 (44.5%);
combining them gives 47/106 (44.3%). All remain **not decided** with the larger family band.
The Claude-only **unblinded** decision remains undecided and the Codex-only **blinded**
prediction remains refuted under each fixed sensitivity. That is the specified decision
rule, not proof that credit can be assigned unambiguously.

| Full protection [W1] | 3.51 percentage points | 1.49 points | 19.69 points |
| Adverse response [W2] | 2.97 points | 2.30 points | 0.81 points |
| Outside decisive work [W3] | 11.59 points | 3.75 points | 6.90 points |

The pooled rule uses the larger available family band, calculated from exact ratios. That
leaves the full-protection prediction standing and leaves the other two undecided. The
Claude band cannot measure the effect of the project instructions that all its coders saw.
Codex’s single-season band cannot be assumed to bound its behavior everywhere.

The never-reached framing difference is especially revealing: 1.58 points for Claude
**unblinded**, but **26.79 points** for Codex **blinded**. Codex’s S13 positive-framed coder
records no known “no” cases and nine unknown reached statuses; the negative-framed coder
records 15 “no” cases. A small protection-rate band does not make person-finding or intake
status stable. The neutral S13 figures beside Codex’s framing pair are Claude **unblinded**;
there is no neutral Codex S13 pair.

## Agreement, and the S17 check

The pooled analysis matches 604 stories: 77.8% of the 776 found by either coder. Claude
**unblinded** pairs find 258 in common out of 324 (79.6%); Codex **blinded** pairs find 363
out of 470 (77.2%, including its S17 cross-check). These family totals overlap at S17 and
must not be added together. Group/individual and separate/combined harms often remain
unmatched rather than forcing an identity. Own-coder figures retain them.

Pooled percent agreement / chance-adjusted agreement is 90.7% / 0.791 for reached status,
77.2% / 0.690 for the five outcomes, 92.7% / 0.795 for the adverse-response indicator,
88.5% / 0.768 for known decisive-worker indicators, 91.6% / 0.748 for institutional harm
at any point and 80.6% / 0.601 for the account-alone evidence tag. Among pairs both eligible
for the adverse-response prediction, agreement is 91.0% / 0.794. The appendix shows every
field by family and season, contact counts and all four cross-family comparisons.

S17’s same-season comparison is less reassuring than the pooled agreement. Full-protection
headlines are 4/7 = 57.1% for Claude **unblinded**, and 1/8 = 12.5% for Codex **blinded**.
The agreed full-protection difference is 44.6 percentage points, with Claude’s **unblinded**
figure higher. Their own-coder fractions are 5/10 and 5/8 (Claude **unblinded**) versus 6/14 and 1/13
(Codex **blinded**). The five-category outcome agreement in A–CA, A–CB, B–CA and B–CB is
36.4%, 45.5%, 30.0% and 20.0%, respectively. Even the Codex **blinded** pair agrees on
only 41.2% of outcomes here; Claude’s **unblinded** pair agrees on 80.0%. These differences
cannot isolate model-family effects from exposure, and this one season cannot calibrate all
seasons. Substituting Codex’s S17 pair in the pool yields 76/390 = 19.5% full protection,
119/389 = 30.6% adverse response, and 51/117 = 43.6% outside work; pooled decisions stay the same.

## The people who never reached an institution

**Disconfirming test:** look for actual contact in stories coded “no”, distinguish a failed
attempt from choosing not to report, and do not infer a majority from people with unknown
status or from uncounted groups.

Both coders agree on “no” for 69 of 490 stories with known and agreed reached status:
**14.1% pooled** (95% interval 11.3–17.4%). It is 60/231 = 26.0% for Claude **unblinded**
and 9/272 = 3.3% for Codex **blinded**. These are not estimates of how much harm goes
unreported in the population. The broad institution definition includes health care,
churches, employers, platforms and businesses, not just reporting to police. Pooled own
records have 91 unknown statuses on the left and 120 on the right; unknown is never “no”.
The pooled own-coder never-reached fractions are 110/602 (18.3%) and 92/567 (16.2%);
the appendix gives the separate families. The sample checks **12 coded records, six matched stories**, plus the first quoted example
for each of nine reasons. Across all four headline checks, the overlapping sample contains
**36 unique coded records**. It is a chosen contrary-case check, not a random error estimate.

Reasons overlap. The checked examples include the first fiancée’s delayed discovery of
account intrusion; Emma saying “I was so terrified of exposing this person. I never told
anyone” (S10 E4, around 44:23); and a failed attempt: “I tried to call the police, but they
didn’t answer” (S10 E3, around 27:47). The code “earlier disbelief” can refer to family, not
an institution: the S1 panelist describes her mother blaming her. It does not by itself
establish that this caused her not to report. In S8, adults explicitly chose not to report
suspected danger because they lacked proof and feared disproportionate consequences.
CJ in S3 and Zara in S9 expose disagreement about whether contact on behalf of a family or
investigation counted as reaching an institution for that person’s harm.

`others_mentioned` is reported by season and coder in the appendix and
[others_mentioned.json](others_mentioned.json), including exact stated counts, unknowns and
ranges. Entries overlap within seasons, across seasons and sometimes with followed stories;
there is no defensible unique-person total. They record people whose stories were not
followed individually, and do not establish that all of them never sought help.

## Beside the earlier results and the outside-world data

The earlier [response-segment result](../power_vector/RESULTS.md) stays on the record:
31 of 49 agreed response segments, or 63%, were classified appropriate. That test’s
pre-registered institutional-neutralization prediction was refuted. It included one coder
who knew the hypothesis; it should not be called a test with two fully blind coders.

The whole-story result adds that useful responses often failed to add up to full protection.
It does not erase the positive responses. Nor does it establish that most reached stories
contained disbelief or blame: the pooled test of those specified responses is undecided,
and the Claude-only **unblinded** prediction is refuted. Much of the gap between a helpful
response and safety may be delay, incomplete action, ongoing harm or a scope limit rather
than an actively hostile response. The present design does not assign a single cause.

The [outside-world figures already documented](../power_vector/EXTERNAL_DATA.md) remain
beside both: about 69% of sexual assaults and 71% of stalking were unreported to police in
the cited sources, and fewer than one in ten reported sexual assaults led to felony
conviction in the cited approximations. Those figures were checked as recorded there;
this continuation did not recheck their source pages. They keep the large population of
unreported and lost cases in the answer; the podcast figures cannot make those people disappear. Arrest or conviction is not the same
as personal safety, and attrition does not by itself identify bad faith. Their low reporting
rates cannot be substituted for this coding’s broad-institution reached-status fraction.

[SUPPORTED] In these coded stories, full protection was uncommon even where both coders
record an institutional contact. The case for reform can cite that finding alongside
specific documented failures and positive cases. The claim that institutions mostly blame,
dismiss or protect themselves, or that outsiders usually did the decisive protective work,
is not established by the pooled method. The unblinded coding, same-season disagreement,
unknown intake status, chosen corpus and limited framing checks remain the limits on the
strongest finding.
