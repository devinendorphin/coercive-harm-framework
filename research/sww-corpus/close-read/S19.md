**The perpetrator's own account (S19a, P1).** At his parole hearing Morrison said he spent 11–12
hours a day online "to substitute the lack of social interaction… by controlling all
interactions through the internet, by creating these fake profiles… face to face, I
obviously could not exert much control." The parole chair: "You've spent close to 30 minutes
telling me about [yourself]… you terrorized someone for your own joy." He called prison "the
greatest experience of my life". This is the corpus's rare first-person statement of
**control as motive**. It comes in his clinicians' vocabulary and from a non-cycle case, so it
supports the construct without extending it to the cycle.

## Target responses (T-fields)

- **Self-documentation against gaslighting (S19b).** Voice memos ("for the record, I am
  completely sober") and talking points written before any conversation: "you should never
  have to go into a conversation with your partner with a note app." Compare S15.
- **External checks of perception (S19b).** She had a coworker listen to the video ("100%
  another woman") and a friend search Tinder.
- **Investigation by the family (S19a).** The sister traced the profiles' followers and found
  another victim; the FBI told her to stop because she was close. The father "almost pegged a
  person in California, which was the wrong person" (divergence 2).
- **The disclosure burden at work (S19a).** Amy told her business partners before their firm
  launched; the PR firm's staff took posts down at night for two years: "a full-time second
  job."
- **Survivors' self-explanations come from therapy and social media** ("narcissist" lists,
  DARVO, the slot machine, the lever-pressing mouse). Recorded as the targets' vocabulary, not
  as findings; coders score conduct.
- **Re-education as protective (S19c).** "Jennifer, you were the victim of domestic violence"
  — she had not known it applied without marriage or children.

## Divergences (recorded, not smoothed)

1. **Control as motive, in the perpetrator's own words (S19a).** See above. A P1 datum that
   fits the construct, from outside the cycle class, in borrowed clinical language.
2. **Profiling by content failed, and amateur identification nearly named the wrong man
   (S19a).** The family expected a white neo-Nazi from the racist content; he was a Latino
   man. The father had "almost pegged" a man in California. **This bears on X3 and on any
   mechanism that relies on victim-led identification**: the conduct was real and the
   inference from it to a person was wrong.
3. **The "jealous" woman was a victim, and the smear-runner became one (S19b).** Doug's
   Memphis "landlady, jealous because I turned her down" was his live-in partner, who said
   she had thrown him out after he raped her. "April", who ran the smear campaign against
   Sarah, later sent Sarah photographs of her own bruises. **The flying-monkey role is a
   position someone occupies, not a kind of person**; behaviour, not people, applies to the
   network too.
4. **Rules meant to protect virtue silenced the reporter (S19c).** The honour code made her
   disclosure an admission; church discipline fell on her while he was given charge of boys.
   Compare S5 (a church that protected its pastor) and S13 (purity culture).
5. **The speech boundary moved during the season (S19e).** Lenora Claire raised *Counterman
   v. Colorado*, 600 U.S. 66 (2023) [ESTABLISHED]: a true-threats prosecution must prove at
   least **recklessness** about the threatening character of the statements. She fears it
   protects stalkers who believe in a relationship. Whether erotomanic belief can negate
   recklessness is an open question this file cannot settle; **doc 07 prong 5 needs to cite
   Counterman**.
6. **Help depended on status or on a lucky call (S19a, S19e).** A duty agent who listened;
   a business partner who pushed; a congressman; a 17-million-viewer special; a unit "really
   only for celebrities". **Prong 6 (disparate impact) applies to wealth and visibility as
   well as race.**
7. **A production created evidence and could have damaged it (S19e).** A true-crime show
   interviewed her stalker without her consent; the footage made the public believe her. She
   also describes a production that filmed co-complainants of an unadjudicated case together,
   handing the defence a contamination argument (she cites the Cosby complainants being kept
   apart). **This sharpens S18's divergence 4: pattern evidence is worth what its
   independence is worth, and publicity can spend it.**
8. **The counsellor who refused the label was right about the label (S19b).** "You can't call
   someone a narcissist" is this framework's own rule. His failure was the other question:
   "What did you do to deserve it?"
9. **Three uses of a diagnosis.** Morrison described his OCD while saying he took "full
   responsibility"; Jennifer's husband blamed Graves' disease for his outbursts; Lenora Claire
   named her stalker's diagnoses with a disclaimer against stigma. **The codebook codes none
   of them.** It codes the claim made as an excuse (S19d, as in S18), never the condition.
10. **Practitioners widen the definitions (S19c).** "Infidelity is a form of domestic
    violence"; "being stalked with no contact is still domestic violence." Recorded as
    professional framing. **It fails the vagueness prong if anyone proposes it as a legal
    definition.**
11. **Theories about the targets (S19e, S18).** "Extra sparkly" victims; "highly empathetic"
    doulas; "he has a type." Recorded as the speakers' hypotheses. The codebook does not code
    a target's traits as a cause.
12. **A record kept without a prosecution (S14 update, S19c).** Kaelin filed a rape report
    "so that there is a history of it"; Jennifer's case sits "on file forever". Records like
    these protect a later victim. Held by police and never tested, they could also be used
    against a person who did nothing. **Both-victims lens: doc 04 should say how an untested
    record may and may not be used.**
13. **The pattern threshold varies (S19b vs S17).** Five women were "not enough" for a
    Missouri prosecutor; in S17 one earlier victim's injuries were admitted as prior-act
    evidence. Recorded; this file cannot say which was right.
