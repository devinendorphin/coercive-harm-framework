# Research: Meidung (Anabaptist/Amish shunning) — anthropology deep-dive

*Source of record for docs/11 Part A "Meidung" entry. Session: /anthropology meidung,

The second answer turned out to be strongly supported.*

## 1. What meidung is (mechanics) [ESTABLISHED]

**Bann vs. Meidung.** The *Bann* is the excommunication status (being "in the Bann");
*Meidung* is the social avoidance that follows — restricting ordinary social contact,
notably not sharing a table or accepting goods/services from the banned member. The two
run sequentially: loss of membership triggers the altered conduct toward the person.

**The graduated pre-excommunication process** (Kraybill; Hurst & McConnell, *An Amish
Paradox*; Hostetler):
1. **Private admonition** — leaders visit to discuss the breach (usually of the
   *Ordnung*), tracking Matthew 18:15–17 (private → witnesses → "tell it to the church").
2. **Grace period** — commonly "six to twelve months" (Holmes County data) to change.
3. **Congregational vote** — "There must be a unanimous vote from the congregation for
   an individual to be excommunicated." The bishop raises it but does not decide alone.
4. Predicate: at baptism members vow to uphold the *Ordnung* and accept the consequences
   of breaking it — the discipline is framed as enforcing a *freely-taken adult vow*.

**Reintegration path** [ESTABLISHED as available; rare in practice]. A banned person may
return by demonstrating "a contrite heart," making a public confession (to the bishop or
kneeling before the congregation), asking forgiveness, and re-committing to the
*Ordnung*; the church then votes to restore. Return is described as genuine cause for
communal joy (the lost-sheep parable). But sources agree it is "not too common" once a
person has left, and in **strict (streng) Meidung** communities (Lancaster, Swiss,
Swartzentruber) the ban is **lifelong unless** the person returns to an Amish church and
confesses. Milder Midwestern affiliations lift the ban if the person joins a related
Anabaptist body (Beachy Amish, progressive Mennonite). So reversibility is **real but
affiliation-dependent and, in the strict form, thin**.

**Stated intent = redemptive** [ESTABLISHED as intent]. Framed as "tough love" to save
the deviant's soul and win the member back, not (in self-understanding) as punishment.
Outsiders and critics observe punitive *effect*; the intent/effect gap is the whole
interpretive fight.

## 2. The disconfirming finding #1: meidung does NOT test genuine vs. performed contrition

This is the correction that matters most for the framework. docs/11 proposed meidung as
a model for distinguishing genuine from performed contrition. **It is not one.** The
restoration standard is *public confession + submission to the Ordnung + visible
behavioral change*. No source describes any mechanism for verifying interior sincerity;
the test is outward compliance over time. Hostetler's own anecdote — a family seating a
banned member at a table pushed within inches of the members' table and covered by a
single tablecloth, so "only the adults knew what had transpired" — shows that even
**symbolic** compliance can satisfy the form. Meidung therefore *demands* a performance of
contrition and accepts the performance; it is the framework's **problem in miniature, not
its solution.** What travels is the *procedural scaffolding* (graduated admonition →
unanimous congregational adjudication → defined restoration ritual), not any contrition
epistemology.

→ This directly answers docs/11 Open Question 1 in the negative: a major tradition with an
explicit reintegration path still had *no validated way* to distinguish performed from
genuine contrition. It settled for time-under-observation + public submission — evidence
that time-under-observation may be the only known test.

## 3. The disconfirming finding #2: meidung as documented coercive control [SUPPORTED]

The sharpest result. Meidung is documented — by ex-Amish advocates and survivor
organizations — as *itself* a coercive-control instrument, i.e., the exact harm this
framework exists to name:
- Baptized women and girls report being **threatened with shunning/excommunication if
  they report rape, molestation, or abuse to outside (English) law enforcement**;
  survivors have been shunned or excommunicated while perpetrators were not (Amish
  Heritage Foundation / Torah Bontrager; Survivors.org; Lancaster Online / PA Family
  Support Alliance reporting).
- Because perpetrators can *perform* contrition and reintegrate while victims who go
  outside the community are punished for the "sin" of leaving/reporting, the mechanism
  can **invert** — shielding the offender and disciplining the harmed. Advocates label
  mandated shunning "one of the most egregious forms of coercive control."
- Family-splitting: parents cut off from children, financial and psychological pressure,
  loss of one's entire social world are the ordinary stakes of exit.

**Epistemic note:** these are advocacy/survivor sources plus some journalism, not
peer-reviewed prevalence studies — tag [SUPPORTED], not [ESTABLISHED]. But they are
multiply and independently attested and internally consistent, and they are exactly the
failure mode the both-victims lens predicts. Do not soft-pedal them.

## 4. Contested from inception (nuance) [ESTABLISHED]

Strict Meidung was never a tradition-wide consensus. The **1693 Ammann–Reist split** —
which created the Amish as distinct from Mennonites — was *about the severity of social
avoidance itself*. Jakob Ammann demanded total social severance of the banned (not mere
exclusion from communion); Hans Reist and allies held that Meidung as Ammann wanted it
was unscriptural, or at least should **not be forced** on congregations that rejected it.
So the strictest form the framework was tempted to model is the very thing that a large
part of the tradition judged excessive and refused. Both sides argued from scripture
(Ammann: Matthew 18:17, 1 Cor 5:11, 2 Thess 3:14; Reist: a milder reading of the same).

## 5. Part B intervention-landscape axes

| Axis | Meidung (as documented) |
|---|---|
| **Trigger** | Formal, graduated: private admonition → witnesses → congregation → **unanimous vote**. Higher due-process than kunlangeta's consensus-killing. But the "offense" is breach of *Ordnung*, which can include leaving or reporting outside — a capture risk. |
| **Reversibility** | Conditional and defined — but rare in practice; lifelong in strict affiliations absent return + confession. |
| **Theory of change** | **Induced contrition + re-submission.** Explicitly redemptive in intent; coercive in mechanism (total social/economic pressure). |
| **Contrition test** | Public confession + Ordnung-submission + visible behavior change over time. **No sincerity test** — performance suffices (see §2). |
| **Abuse safeguards** | **Weak-to-absent against capture.** No external appeal; the community holds a disciplinary monopoly, which is precisely what lets it be turned against abuse-reporters (§3). |
| **Scale** | Congregation / affiliation. Depends on a closed reputation economy and high exit cost. |
| **Documented failure modes** | Weaponization to silence abuse victims; shielding of contrition-performing perpetrators; family separation; punishment of exit rather than of harm. |

## 6. Witch-trial / weaponization audit (feeds docs/07)

Meidung is a **dual-use** anchor and, on the evidence, more cautionary than exemplary. Its
procedural front-end (graduated admonition, unanimous adjudication, a defined restoration
ritual) is genuinely more calibrated than kunlangeta's covert killing. But its back-end
has no external escape valve, and the documented result is capture: the disciplinary
machinery turned against the people it should protect. The governing lesson for the
framework: **any restorative/rehabilitative track must have an external, non-disciplinable
escape valve** — access to state law enforcement and courts that the community cannot
punish a member for using. A restoration ritual without that valve reproduces meidung's
failure.
